Content example audit

Can we find and curate content? Yes. We looked at two segments of communicators, health professionals and outdoor/athlete voices, and found and graded 19 pieces across videos, social posts, articles and op-eds against the Eight Principles for Effective and Inviting Climate Communication.

This is a first-pass screen, not a full line-by-line review. Several pages showed only an excerpt and required human effort to acquire the full content. We would need human reviews before any piece goes into the library.

Grading key

Each piece gets one mark per principle.

  • +Strong. Clearly does what the principle asks.
  • ~Mixed. Partly there, or one fixable miss.
  • –Weak. Works against the principle.
  • ·Not in play for this piece.

Verdicts

  • Good exampleMostly +, no –.
  • So-soUseful to teach a fix.
  • Not yet usableToo thin to grade.

Health professionals

What makes each one land or miss

  • Howard is the model for the pilot. She names local towns and admits her own home runs four air filters, then gives three steps anyone can take today. Her one slip is jargon about particle size in microns.
  • Merritt opens with a patient in her 70s during the heat dome and says plainly he is a doctor, not an activist. That protects his standing. He calls it the first time he wrote the term, which makes the practice sound rare.
  • Dresser turns climate into a clinical question: "What's your plan?" for a hot day. It is the clearest doable step in the set, but it dates from 2021.
  • Vossler has a powerful patient story, but the headline turns it into a fight about the President. Readers who don't share that view stop reading, and the excerpt offers no path forward.
  • The nursing piece shows real collective momentum (26 organizations, 5 million nurses). It is buried in agency acronyms and a long list of asks with no first step.
  • The Seattle op-ed by three Washington physicians has some of the most vivid patient stories in the set, such as a man who had a heart attack waiting for roadside help on I-5. But it opens with a long list of disasters and "despair and hopelessness," calls science "no longer a matter of debate," and ends with more than ten asks at once. It pairs well with Lawson's speech on the same 2021 heat dome.

Added from social media

  • Lawson's heat-dome speech is the best health piece in the set. She worked downtown Vancouver during the 2021 heat dome, then moves from grief to cheaper, cleaner energy and asks people to check on isolated seniors. It had 20 views.
  • The Medical Society of Virginia short is a peer-to-peer model: ask older patients about heat and asthma parents about smoky air. It shows how clinicians can make climate part of routine care. It had 55 views.
  • The WHO TikTok is a chorus of health workers at COP28, each naming what they see in their own patients: dust storms, breathing problems, heart risk, longer pollen seasons. Many voices make speaking up feel normal, and the WHO name adds trust. It ends on alarm with no step for viewers. It is the only vertical, phone-first piece in the set and a model for communicators who speak for an organization. About 57,000 plays and 219 likes when checked on Nov 21, 2026.
  • Instagram search found no active clinician accounts. Health voices sit on YouTube and LinkedIn, which matches the research.

What makes each one land or miss

  • Diggins wants future kids to ski, hike and breathe clean air, and says she wants that "very badly." The team frame and hopeful tone are strong. The article then pulls the story into a fight over the current EPA.
  • The Senate testimony has vivid stories: Jones watching glaciers retreat, Caldwell in the Arctic Refuge. But athletes present a "field report" like experts, which Principle 7 warns against. It also mentions denial and was delivered to a single-party committee.
  • Gleich's profile is upbeat about "showing up and speaking out" but has no personal story and no specific step. She later ran for the U.S. Senate as a Democrat, which may limit her reach with conservative audiences.
  • Suter is the only athlete piece strong on all eight principles. He talks about learning, not lecturing: a beach cleanup changed his habits, his household now composts, and his Brewers teammates cut plastic use in half. He gives fans one concrete step (try composting, with a how-to link) and praises bipartisan action. Caveats: it dates from 2020 and is mostly about plastic waste, with climate only at the end.
  • The EcoAthletes page is a roster, not a message. It is useful for recruiting, not as a library example.

Added from social media

  • POW's "signs are everywhere" post asks followers to text three friends, a small and specific step framed as what everyone is already doing.
  • Diggins' race post mentions choosing a race she can reach on electric mode. It normalizes clean driving without preaching, though it is a sponsored Toyota post.
  • Diggins' "I voted" post has a strong personal reason and an easy step. It also leads with 11.2 million environmentalists not voting, which tells readers the behavior is rare. It is the clearest P6 teaching example we have.
  • POW's Arctic post gives a clear action (submit a public comment) but centers a place, not people, and shows no one else acting.
  • POW's post-election celebration rallies the base but reads as a partisan win. Its comments filled with energy and EV arguments.
  • Brent Suter's Instagram has not posted since 2024, so his feed is too stale to use.

Across both segments

Patterns and what they mean for the platform

The same grading works for both segments, and both miss in the same two places: no doable step for the audience (P4) and political framing (P8). That makes one shared rubric viable, with segment-specific guidance layered on top.

Two platform needs already

  • Full-text capture. Four of 11 sources gave only an excerpt or teaser through public pages. Ingestion must reach full articles, transcripts and social posts, or grading will skew.
  • Contrast pairs. The most teachable units are a strong and a so-so piece on the same topic, such as Howard vs. Vossler on heat and smoke. These suit training modules and LLM prompts alike.

Two more from social media

  • Distribution, not just curation. The best health videos had 20 and 55 views. Good content exists but no one sees it, so the platform should help messengers reuse and repost it.
  • Sponsorship and comment context. Athlete posts are often sponsored, and climate posts attract argument in the comments. Library entries should flag both so messengers know what they are sharing.
