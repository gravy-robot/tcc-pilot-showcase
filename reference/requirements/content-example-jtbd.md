# Content Example: Jobs to Be Done

**Project:** TCC Curated Content Platform, health professionals pilot
**Author:** TCC Design Research
**Date:** November 9, 2026
**Status:** Draft for review

**Sources:**
- TCC and American University landscape assessments (2026)
- TCC segment example audit (Nov 2026), `reference/audit.md`
- Health-professional journey map and touchpoint map (Dr. Maya persona), `reference/persona-and-stages.md`

---

## 1. Purpose

This brief defines the jobs a **Content Example** must do to help trusted messengers become confident, effective climate communicators. Those jobs set the requirements for one component that works across the whole journey (`content-example-requirements.md`).

**What a Content Example is:** any curated piece of real climate communication that TCC shows a messenger. It can appear as:
- a single piece in the library
- one half of a contrast pair (a strong piece and a weaker one)
- a reference the messenger's AI chatbot uses as a model
- an attribution on the messenger's own post

**Who it serves first:** health professionals, represented by Dr. Maya, a composite family physician. She has little time and is wary of anything that looks partisan. She posts on LinkedIn, drafts in an AI chatbot and makes handouts in Canva.

---

## 2. The core job

> **When** I've decided climate belongs in my professional voice,
> **I want to** see someone like me doing it well,
> **so I can** do it myself without risking my credibility.

**Why examples, rather than training, are at the center:**
- In the TCC/AU research, communicators ranked regular updates on "what works" (54%) far above training (27%) and science education (24%).
- The research also notes that findings rarely reach working professionals such as a busy doctor.
- Dr. Maya's voice on the journey map: "Show me good examples. Don't lecture me."

Being **confident** and being **effective** are separate outcomes. An example has to build both: belief that she *can* and *should* speak, and the skill to speak *well*.

## 3. Jobs by journey stage

Each job is written as a job story. "Practical" is what she gets done; "emotional/social" is how she feels and how she looks to others.

### J1 · Stage 1: Notice. *Give her permission.*
> When climate shows up in my exam room as heat and smoke, I want to see a fellow clinician talk about it practically, so I know it's appropriate for someone like me to speak.

- **Practical:** links climate to health effects she already sees.
- **Emotional/social:** "This is normal for doctors." Removes the fear of looking political.
- **Journey evidence:** pain point is that climate content reads as political and she has little time. The example in the map is Howard on wildfire smoke.

### J2 · Stage 2: Explore. *Signal fit fast.*
> When I'm looking for guidance, I want to tell within seconds whether something is for clinicians or for activists, so I don't waste time or pick up a partisan frame.

- **Practical:** filter by role, topic, place and how recent it is.
- **Emotional/social:** trust. It reads like a clinical source, not a campaign.
- **Journey evidence:** nothing currently filters by profession, topic or place. The example in the map is Lawson's heat-dome speech as the hero of a health library.

### J3 · Stage 3: Join. *Preview the value.*
> When I'm asked to sign up, I want to see the examples I'll get, so I know the commitment is worth it.

- **Practical:** the payoff is visible before she commits.
- **Emotional/social:** low risk. "I can give this five minutes a week."
- **Journey evidence:** sign-ups ask a lot and promise vague benefits. The offer in the map is "Get 3 patient-ready examples."

### J4 · Stage 4: Learn. *Make "good" visible.*
> When I'm learning what works, I want to compare a strong example with a weaker one and see why, so I can recognize good communication on my own.

- **Practical:** teaches the Eight Principles through real cases rather than lessons.
- **Emotional/social:** confidence. "I can tell the difference now."
- **Journey evidence:** contrast pairs Lawson vs. Seattle op-ed, and Howard vs. Vossler.

### J5 · Stage 5: Create. *Be a model she can borrow.*
> When I'm drafting a post, op-ed or handout, I want a proven model to borrow the structure and tone from, so my draft is fast, sounds like me and avoids jargon, doom and partisanship.

- **Practical:** reusable structure (hook, local link, one doable step) that her AI chatbot or Canva template can follow.
- **Emotional/social:** "I don't want to sound like a doomer, or partisan."
- **Journey evidence:** AI drafts come back generic, jargon-heavy or doom-laden. The research shows alarming messages raise concern but erode efficacy, the belief that action matters.

### J6 · Stage 6: Share. *Provide cover.*
> When I'm about to publish, I want evidence that respected peers have said something similar safely, so I and my employer's comms office can approve it with confidence.

- **Practical:** precedent that makes internal approval easier.
- **Emotional/social:** "I'm not the first."
- **Journey evidence:** employer gatekeepers can approve or block a post.

### J7 · Stage 7: Return and connect. *Connect her to peers and credit her work.*
> When I've shared my piece, I want to see it make a difference and find others like me, so I keep going and don't do this alone.

- **Practical:** her piece can become an example for others, which gets it seen and gets her feedback.
- **Emotional/social:** belonging. Most communicators work alone, and 57% want help finding peers and collaborators.
- **Journey evidence:** the contribution loop and monthly digest in the map.

## 4. Jobs for other stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Job the Content Example does for them |
|---|---|
| **TCC** | A unit of evidence about "what works," and something to amplify. In the audit, the strongest health videos had only 20 and 55 views, a distribution problem curation can fix. |
| **Original creator** | Credit, attribution and a wider audience for work they've already done. |
| **Gatekeepers** (hospital comms, medical societies) | A precedent they can approve against. One approval can be reused across a health system. |
| **AI tools** (the messenger's chatbot) | A structured, annotated reference that steers drafts toward the Eight Principles. |

---

## 5. Underlying needs, across all stages

1. **Relevance:** "Is this someone like me, about my patients, in my place?"
2. **Credibility:** "Is it sourced, non-partisan and from a trusted institution?"
3. **Clarity about why it works:** "What exactly makes this good?"
4. **Something reusable:** "What can I take from it?"
5. **Safety:** "Will this hurt my job or my patients' trust?"
6. **Connection:** "Who else is doing this?"

---

## 6. Evidence caveats

- **The sample was different.** The AU survey sampled professional climate communicators, not trusted messengers like clinicians. The "examples over training" finding fits Dr. Maya's persona, but it is borrowed evidence.
- **Dr. Maya is a composite.** Her voice lines are illustrative, not interview quotes.
- **What to test first:** the jobs at stages 4 to 6 (learn, create, share) carry the most design weight and the least direct evidence. Test them in clinician interviews before locking in requirements.
